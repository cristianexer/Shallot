How the network keeps a source anonymous

By the Security Desk · 12 min read · updated today

Every request you make here is wrapped in three layers of encryption and bounced through volunteers around the world before it reaches us. Here is what that actually protects — and what it doesn’t.

Fig 1 — Traffic leaves your device already encrypted three times over.

When you open a page over an anonymity network, your device does not talk to the destination directly. It builds a path through three separate relays. The first knows who you are but not where you are going. The last knows the destination but not who sent the request. No single point on the path can see both ends at once.

That separation is the whole point. A network operator watching your connection sees only that you are talking to the first relay, and even that looks like ordinary encrypted noise. Your address, your location, and the sites you visit stay held apart from one another.

Why the entry point matters most

The first relay — the guard — is the one part of the path your device keeps for a while rather than swapping on every request. Holding it steady is deliberate: rotating it constantly would give an observer more chances to notice you, not fewer. It is a rare case where doing something less often is the safer choice.

“The system is not trying to hide that you are using it. It is trying to make sure that using it tells no one anything useful.”

Each relay peels off one layer of encryption, like the layers of an onion, and passes the request along. By the time it arrives, nothing in the request points back to you. The replies travel the same path in reverse, and are reassembled only on your device.

Fig 2 — Each hop removes exactly one layer and learns nothing about the others.

What it protects, and what it can’t

Anonymity at the network layer is strong, but it is not the same as being invisible. A few things still deserve care:

  • Logging in to a personal account links this session to your real identity — the network cannot undo that.
  • Files opened outside the browser may reach out over an ordinary connection and reveal your address.
  • The way a browser renders a page can be measured, so the more it blends in with everyone else, the better.

Used carefully, though, the result is genuinely powerful: a way to read, research, and send information without handing over who you are or where you sit. The rest is a matter of habit — and of trusting the layers to do their quiet work.
